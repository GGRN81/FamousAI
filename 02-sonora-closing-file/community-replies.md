## Reply 1 — "Is a fideicomiso safe? Do I really own it?"

> You own contractual rights — use, enjoyment, and the right to sell — and the bank holds
> title. That sounds worse than it is: the trustee cannot sell, encumber or use the property,
> and it must follow your written instructions. In practice it functions like ownership with
> a custodian and an annual fee.
>
> The three things that actually matter, and none of them are about safety:
>
> The trust has a term, and someone has to renew it. Find out who, in writing.
>
> There is a substitute beneficiary line. If it is blank when you die, your heirs open a
> Mexican succession proceeding. If it is filled in with correctly spelled names, the transfer
> is administrative.
>
> If you bought rights from a previous foreign owner, confirm with the bank — not the seller —
> that the beneficiary substitution was actually issued. A private assignment does not change
> the bank's records.

## Reply 2 — "Should I buy through a Mexican corporation instead?"

> It depends on three things, and anyone who answers without asking them is guessing.
>
> How many properties. One property, personal use, long hold: the trust is usually simpler and
> cheaper over the life of the asset. Several properties, or a portfolio: the corporation
> starts to win.
>
> Whether you will rent it. Regular rental income pushes toward a corporation, and it changes
> your filing obligations in Mexico either way.
>
> How you plan to exit, and what happens if you die first. This is where the two paths differ
> most, and it is the part almost nobody models. The trust has the substitute beneficiary
> shortcut. The corporation does not — the shares pass through succession like any other
> asset, which may be fine if your estate planning already accounts for it, and is a problem
> if it does not.
>
> A corporation also carries maintenance most buyers do not expect: annual meetings, corporate
> books, tax filings, and beneficial-owner records. It is not a set-and-forget vehicle.

## Reply 3 — "Seller says the property was ejido but it's regularized now"

> Then there is a document that proves it, and you should be holding a copy before you make an
> offer.
>
> Ejido land is communal agrarian property. It can be brought into the ordinary property
> system, but it is a formal process with a paper trail, and the trail either exists or it
> does not. There is no in-between and there is no verbal version.
>
> Ask for the instrument that transformed it and the current registry certificate, and check
> that the property described in the certificate is the same one you are being shown. That
> last part sounds obvious and it is where a surprising number of these break.
>
> If the answer is "everyone here bought that way", that is a statement about local custom,
> not about whether the title can be conveyed to a foreign national.

## Reply 4 — "What taxes will I pay when I sell?"

> The important part is not the rate. It is that the documentation determining how the tax is
> computed has to exist *before* you sell, and it can only be assembled while you still own
> the property. ⚠ Rates and mechanics change; confirm against the statute in force at the time.
>
> Broadly: whether you have a Mexican tax ID and a designated legal representative changes how
> the withholding is calculated on a sale by a foreign national. There are also exemptions tied
> to residence that require proof accumulated over time, not produced at the closing table.
>
> The practical advice is boring and it is the whole thing: decide on the day you buy how you
> expect to exit, and put the paperwork in place then. Everyone who tells me they wish they had
> done something differently is talking about this.

## Reply 5 — "My closing is Friday and something feels off"

> Ask for three documents before you release funds, and you can ask for all of them today:
>
> A public registry certificate issued within the last thirty days. Not the copy in the file —
> a current one. An older certificate describes the past.
>
> A written statement from the trustee bank of who the beneficiary of record is today.
>
> The property tax and water certificates showing no arrears.
>
> If any of the three cannot be produced before Friday, that is information. It does not
> necessarily mean the deal is bad. It means the date is doing work that the documents are not
> ready to support, and moving a closing date is far cheaper than unwinding one.
