What actually goes wrong

Six failures I see
over and over

None of these are exotic. Every one of them is ordinary, and every one of them is discovered years later — at the sale, or at a death.

01

The seller is not the owner of record

Chain of title broke three transfers back, or the land came from an ejido — communal agrarian property — and was never properly regularized. It cannot be conveyed to a foreign national at all.

02

The beneficiary substitution was never recorded

The prior buyer "assigned rights" in a private document nobody took to the trustee bank. The bank's records still show someone else. That person can instruct the trustee. You cannot.

03

No substitute beneficiary designated

The single most skipped line in the entire trust. Leave it blank and your heirs open a Mexican succession proceeding for a beach house. Fill it in at signing and the transfer is administrative.

04

No Mexican tax ID, no representative, at the exit

Withholding on the sale is computed one way with the documentation, another way without it. The paperwork can only be assembled while you still own the property. ⚠ rate and mechanics verified against the statute in force

05

Expired trust term, or a permit that no longer matches

The bank trust has a term and somebody has to renew it. The Foreign Affairs permit was issued for a stated use that may not be what you are actually doing with the property.

06

The wrong vehicle entirely

You were sold a bank trust when your case called for a Mexican corporation, or the reverse. That decision is made once and paid for permanently.

The report

The twelve points
I check on your file

  • Restricted zone. Whether the property is actually inside it, measured, not assumed
  • Owner of record. Whether the seller is who the registry says owns it
  • Chain of title. Three transfers back, with the gaps named
  • Ejido origin. Whether the land was ever properly regularized
  • Trust term. When it expires and whose job renewal is
  • Beneficiary substitution. Whether yours was ever recorded with the trustee
  • Substitute beneficiary. What happens to the property when you die
  • Permit scope. Whether the SRE permit matches your intended use
  • Vehicle choice. Bank trust versus Mexican corporation, for your specific case
  • Encumbrances. Liens, tax certificates, condominium regime, HOA obligations
  • Exit position. Your Mexican tax ID posture before you need it
  • The gaps. The documents your closing packet does not contain
A note on vocabulary. A fideicomiso is a Mexican bank trust: the bank holds title, you hold contractual rights of use, enjoyment and sale. It is not a common-law trust and it does not behave like one. Every civil-law term in your report is glossed in half a line the first time it appears. You should not need a second attorney to read the first one.

Questions

Before you order

Are you my attorney?

Not from this report alone. This is a documentary review delivered as a written opinion, with its scope and limitations stated on page one. If you want representation in a transaction, that is a separate written engagement and it is priced separately.

Can you guarantee the title is clean?

No, and nobody honest can. I tell you what the documents you gave me show, what they do not show, and what I could not verify. A large part of the value is the second list.

My realtor says everything is fine.

It usually is, in the sense that the transaction will close. The failures in this practice are not closing failures; they are exit failures and inheritance failures, and they surface five to fifteen years later when the person who assured you is no longer involved.

What documents do you need?

Whatever you have: the trust agreement, the deed or draft deed, the registry certificate, the purchase agreement, the closing statement, HOA documents. If you have nothing yet because you are still considering, order anyway — the pre-offer version is the cheapest moment to ask these questions.

Do you handle the closing itself?

No. In Mexico a notary — a specially licensed attorney holding public faith — authorizes the instrument. I do the review and the structuring. That separation is deliberate: where I am paid to structure, a different notary certifies.

How do I pay?

Card, in U.S. dollars, on a flat fee confirmed in writing before work starts. Invoice provided.
